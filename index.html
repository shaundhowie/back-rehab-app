Safety & how to progress

Get urgent medical care (emergency department) if you have: Numbness in the groin, inner thighs or around the back passage ("saddle" area) · New trouble controlling your bladder or bowels · Rapidly worsening weakness in a leg or foot drop · Back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, or after a significant fall or accident.
See your GP or a physio soon if: Leg pain is severe and not improving after 2–3 weeks · You notice weakness (e.g. catching your toe, foot slapping) · Pain wakes you every night · Symptoms are in both legs.

Pain traffic light

0–3 / 10 — Green. Fine to continue. Mild stretch or effort is normal.

4–5 / 10 — Amber. Ease off: fewer reps, smaller range, shorter holds.

6+ / 10, or symptoms spreading down the leg — Red. Stop that exercise today. Retry it gently tomorrow; if it keeps happening, drop it and ask a physio.

Key principles

Nerves like movement, not stretching. Nerve glides should be smooth and easy, never held or pushed into strong tingling.

Centralisation is your compass. If an exercise makes leg pain retreat toward your back, it's usually the right one for you — even if back pain briefly feels stronger.

Little and often beats hard and rare. Doing the routine daily (or twice daily in Phase 1) matters more than intensity.

Keep walking. Short, frequent walks are one of the best things for sciatica. Avoid long periods of sitting — get up every 30–45 minutes.

Next-day check. It's normal to feel muscles working. It's not okay if you're clearly worse the next morning — scale back.

Building a good workout

A simple order that works: 1) mobility (pelvic tilts, cat–cow) → 2) nerve glide → 3) strength & core → 4) stretches → 5) finish with a walk.

If you're in a flare-up: keep it short and gentle — pelvic tilts, cat–cow, nerve glide, and either press-ups or knee-to-chest (whichever centralises your symptoms), then a walk.

As things settle: add side planks and bird dogs plus glute bridges and dead bugs. Increase holds or reps only when today's numbers feel easy and you're fine the next morning.

Yoga & stretches: great for flexibility and winding down. Back-bending poses (sphinx, cobra-style) suit most disc-related sciatica; forward folds (seated and standing fold) are the ones most likely to stir it up — bend your knees and skip them if leg symptoms increase.

Mobility vs stretching: mobility drills are active — you move a joint through its range under control. They make an ideal warm-up (5–10 minutes) before strength work or a walk. Save longer static stretches for the end or for winding down.

Agility (skipping & ladders): these are impact drills. Only add them once you can walk briskly for 30 minutes and do the strength work without pain or leg symptoms. Start with short rounds, land softly, and stop if anything jars your back or sends pain down the leg.

Make two or three workouts — e.g. a 5-minute "Desk break", a full "Morning routine" and a gentle "Bad day" version — so you always have something that suits how your back feels.
